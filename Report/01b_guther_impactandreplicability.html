Study 2: Journal-Level Citation Metrics and Replicability

To examine whether journal-level citation impact (OpenAlex Mean Citedness, OMC) is associated with replication success, we conducted an exploratory study of secondary data. We modelled replication outcome as a binary variable (0 = failure, 1 = success) and used the log-transformed OMC as the predictor. Analyses were conducted separately for the most recent full FLoRA database and the GutheR subsample. For each dataset we (a) verified the logistic regression linearity-in-log-odds assumption via the Box–Tidwell test, (b) fitted a logistic regression with log-transformed OMC, (c) fitted a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) to capture potential non-linear effects, and (d) tested whether scientific discipline confounds the OMC–replicability relationship using mixed-effects logistic regression with a random intercept for discipline. Because the Box–Tidwell test indicated a remaining violation of the linearity assumption even after log-transformation in the GutheR subsample, we additionally fitted a Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) logistic regression for that dataset. The analyses were not preregistered.

Descriptive Overview

The distributions of OMC values were similar across both datasets, with the majority of studies falling in the range 0 < OMC < 8 (see Figure @ref(fig:omc-plot-hist)).

Results

Across both datasets, higher OMC was negatively associated with replication success: studies published in journals with higher mean citedness were somewhat less likely to be successfully replicated.

FLoRA. In the FLoRA database (N = 1623; 806 successes, 709 failures; uninformative and mixed studies were excluded), the Box–Tidwell test confirmed that the log-transformation was sufficient to satisfy the linearity-in-log-odds assumption. Logistic regression with log-transformed OMC yielded a statistically significant overall model fit (\(\chi^2\)(1) = 13.21, p < .001). Model performance was, however, negligible: McFadden’s \(R^2\) = 0.006 (logistic regression) and 0.016 (GAM); AUC = 0.56 and 0.58, respectively. The predicted curves are shown in Figure @ref(fig:omc-plot-lr-FLoRA); the OMC threshold at which predicted replication success equalled 50% was 5.53.

GutheR. In the GutheR subsample (N = 1234; 624 successes, 547 failures; uninformative and mixed studies were excluded), the Box–Tidwell test indicated a remaining violation of the linearity assumption even after log-transformation, motivating the additional RCS model. Overall logistic regression fit was again statistically significant (\(\chi^2\)(1) = 12.42, p < .001). Across all three model specifications, performance remained negligible (McFadden’s \(R^2\): logistic = 0.008, GAM = 0.013, RCS = 0.013; AUC: logistic = 0.56, GAM = 0.56, RCS = 0.57), as shown in Figure @ref(fig:omc-plot-guther). The RCS model located the P(success) = 0.50 threshold at an OMC of 3.08, though wide confidence intervals limit the precision of this estimate.

Role of scientific discipline. Multilevel logistic regression with a random intercept for scientific discipline revealed that discipline accounts for a small share of variance in replication outcomes in both datasets (ICCFLoRA = 0.046, ICCGutheR = 0.052), indicating that OMC and discipline are partially confounded. Nevertheless, OMC retained a small but statistically significant independent effect in both datasets after accounting for disciplinary clustering (FLoRA: \(\hat\beta\) = -0.34, p < .001; GutheR: \(\hat\beta\) = -0.4, p < .001). The overlap between the two datasets and the distribution of OMC values are shown in Figure @ref(fig:omc-plot-compare). Model diagnostics, including DHARMa residual simulations, overdispersion tests, outlier tests, and perfect-separation checks, indicated no violations of modelling assumptions in either dataset.

Discussion

Taken together, while the negative effect of OMC on replication success is statistically detectable and robust across model specifications and datasets, its explanatory power is too limited for journal-level citation metrics to serve as a meaningful heuristic for judging the replicability of individual studies.
